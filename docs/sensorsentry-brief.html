Every serious answer to GNSS spoofing today is a hardware answer — controlled-reception antennas, multi-frequency receivers, chip-level fixes. That protects the fleet you buy next year and does nothing for the one already flying. SensorSentry is the software answer: a trust layer that runs on the flight computer that is already there.

The threat

A receiver believes the loudest satellite it can hear

GNSS signals arrive from roughly 20 000 km away at a power below the noise floor. A transmitter on the ground — now buildable for the price of a mid-range laptop — can drown them out and hand the receiver a counterfeit position instead. The receiver has no way to object. It reports the fake fix with full confidence, and every system downstream of it inherits the lie: navigation, geofencing, return-to-home, flight logs, delivery confirmation, insurance evidence.

This has stopped being theoretical. Commercial aviation, shipping and drone operators all now log spoofing and jamming incidents in contested regions, and the equipment cost has fallen far enough that the problem will not stay confined to them.

The countermeasures that work are physical: antenna arrays that reject signals arriving from the wrong direction, receivers that cross-check multiple frequency bands. They are effective, and they are the reason nothing is protected — they cost hundreds to thousands of dollars per airframe and require touching every unit. Fleets that are already in service simply do not get retrofitted.

What is actually new here

Three claims we can defend

The vulnerability is well known and the physics are public. Novelty is not in noticing that GPS can be faked — it is in where the defence sits and what it does once it fires.

Claim 01 · Placement

Defence moves from the antenna to the estimator

We never inspect the radio signal. We ask whether the reported position is consistent with the vehicle's own physics — inertial integration, barometric altitude, magnetic heading. A spoofer can forge a signal; forging a trajectory that satisfies every other sensor simultaneously is a far harder problem. That reframing is what removes the hardware.

Claim 02 · Output

A graded trust score, not a binary alarm

Published work mostly produces spoofed / not-spoofed. Operationally that is close to useless: a false positive that drops navigation mid-flight is as dangerous as the attack. We emit a continuous per-sensor trust value with attribution — which check disagreed, by how much, for how long — so the response can be proportionate.

Claim 03 · Response

Detection wired to graceful degradation

Detecting an attack and then still flying on the spoofed fix is theatre. When trust falls, the estimator down-weights GNSS and holds course on dead reckoning, with an honest and growing error budget shown to the operator. The deliverable is continued safe operation under attack, not a warning light.

Architecture

Three pieces, all software, all on one machine

Test harness

Scenario simulator

Flies a scripted route and emits synthetic GNSS, IMU, barometer and magnetometer streams at realistic rates with realistic noise. Doubles as the attack tool: spoof profiles are injected into the stream on a timeline you control.

The product

Detection engine

Consumes a sensor stream and outputs, every tick, a trust score per sensor plus a ranked list of which consistency checks are failing and by how much. Stateless about where the data came from — a log file and a live feed look identical to it.

Operator surface

Live console

Map with reported track against inertially-estimated track, residual and trust timelines, an evidence panel naming the triggered checks, and the navigation-source indicator flipping from GNSS to dead reckoning.

The seam between the simulator and the engine is the entire commercial argument, so keep it clean and say so out loud: the simulator is our test harness, not our product. The engine takes a standard telemetry stream, so pointing it at a real flight log changes nothing about how it runs.

Detection stack

What can actually be checked

Five independent layers, cheapest first. Independence is the point — an attacker who defeats one still has to satisfy the rest, and the layers fail in different ways, so agreement between them is evidence.

L1

Kinematic plausibility

Bound reported position, velocity and acceleration against what the airframe can physically do. Flag discontinuities in the position sequence and impossible jerk.

Catches Instant teleports and clumsy takeover attacks. Nearly free to compute, near-zero false-positive rate, so it runs first and always.
L2

Inertial divergence

Integrate accelerometer and gyroscope into an independent dead-reckoned position and track the residual against the GNSS fix. IMU drift is unbounded but characterised, so the expected residual envelope grows predictably with time.

Catches The important case — slow walk-off attacks that stay individually plausible. This is the workhorse layer and the one that carries the demo.
L3

Filter innovation monitoring

Inside the extended Kalman filter, the innovation sequence should be zero-mean white noise with a known covariance. Run a normalised innovation squared test against its chi-square bound and watch for sustained bias.

Catches Statistically principled early warning — often trips before the raw residual crosses any threshold, and it produces a calibrated confidence rather than a tuned constant.
L4

Cross-modal consistency

Barometric altitude against GNSS altitude. Magnetometer heading against course-over-ground. Where available, optical flow or wheel odometry against reported ground speed.

Catches Attacks that respect kinematics but ignore the other instruments. Vertical channel is the soft spot — spoofers routinely get altitude wrong, and the barometer is not on their radio.
L5

Receiver-level telemetry

Carrier-to-noise ratio per satellite in dB-Hz, receiver clock bias drift, dilution-of-precision discontinuities, satellite count and geometry changes, and time-of-week consistency.

Catches Signature of the transmitter itself: a spoofer typically arrives louder and more uniform across satellites than a real constellation, and it usually gets the clock wrong.

Fusion and hysteresis

Layer outputs combine into one trust value with evidence weighting, and — this matters more than it sounds — hysteresis. Momentary disagreement during an aggressive turn is not an attack. Confidence must accumulate over a window before the state changes, and decay before it changes back. Without this the system flaps, and a flapping detector gets switched off by its operator within a day.

Scope control

What to build, and what to refuse

Must ship

  • Simulator with one route and three attack profiles
  • L1, L2 and L4 detection layers
  • Fused trust score with hysteresis
  • Dead-reckoning fallback on trust collapse
  • Live console: dual track map, residual plot, evidence panel
  • Measured detection latency and false-positive count on a clean run

If time allows

  • L3 innovation monitoring — best credibility per hour spent
  • L5 receiver telemetry checks
  • Replay of a real public flight log through the engine
  • Exportable incident report bundle
  • Detection-latency curve across attack aggressiveness

Deliberately out

  • Raw RF or signal-level processing — that is the hardware answer we are displacing
  • Any real airframe or receiver
  • Authentication schemes needing constellation-side support
  • A trained neural model where a physics check is stronger and explainable
Optional integration

Where a language model earns its place — and where it must not sit

Nothing in the detection path calls a language model. Spoofing detection is estimation theory; an LLM is the wrong instrument, and saying that plainly is stronger than apologising for a constraint.

It earns a place in exactly one role: translating an incident into a report a human reads. The engine already emits structured evidence — triggered checks, residual magnitudes, timestamps, the trust trajectory. Turning that into "at t=142 s the reported fix diverged 340 m from the inertial estimate while carrier-to-noise rose uniformly across all tracked satellites, consistent with a nearby transmitter overpowering the constellation" is a genuine language task, and it is post-hoc.

Give the console a visible toggle. Turn it off on stage and every detection still fires, identically. That single gesture answers the question every judge is holding — did you just call somebody's API? — better than any slide, and it doubles as the honest engineering argument: an in-loop network call adds a second of latency and a dependency to a safety function that must run at sensor rate, offline, deterministically, and identically every time it is replayed for an investigator.

Commercial case

Who pays, and why the economics work

Software-only means near-zero marginal cost per protected vehicle and no procurement of physical parts — the deciding factor when the buyer already owns the fleet. Willingness to pay is anchored not to the software but to the asset: an aircraft that flies away is a total loss plus a cargo claim plus an incident investigation.

Revenue model

Per-vehicle annual licence for the on-board engine, a fleet subscription for the monitoring console, and a paid forensics tier — signed, replayable incident bundles for insurers and investigators. That third line is the one that compounds: once your incident report is what an insurer accepts as evidence, switching cost stops being technical.

Do not put market-size figures on your slides unless you have verified them yourself. Judges check, and a wrong number costs more credibility than no number at all. The asset-value argument above is stronger anyway, because it is arithmetic they can do in their heads.
Run of show

Ninety seconds, live

  1. 0:00Normal flight. Route running, both tracks overlapping on the map, trust holding at 1.00. Say the one line that frames everything: no extra hardware, running on the sensors this vehicle already has.
  2. 0:15Hard manoeuvre. Bank the aircraft aggressively. Residual twitches; trust dips and recovers; no alarm. "It didn't fire — that's the half everyone skips."
  3. 0:30Attack begins. Trigger the walk-off. Nothing visibly wrong at first, and say so — this is the attack that beats per-sample checks.
  4. 0:45Divergence. The two tracks separate on the map while the residual climbs. Let the room watch it for a beat before the alarm.
  5. 0:55Detection. Trust collapses, evidence panel names the failing checks with magnitudes, navigation source flips to dead reckoning and the vehicle holds its true corridor while the reported fix walks away.
  6. 1:10Numbers. Detection latency, induced error at detection, false positives across the clean run. Three figures on one card.
  7. 1:20The toggle. Disable the language model. Re-run the attack. Identical detection, no report text. "The detection is ours. The model writes the paperwork."
Execution

Stack and sequence

Python for the simulator and engine — NumPy for the filter mathematics, and nothing heavier. A small web console over a websocket feed. No GPU, no training run, no dataset dependency, no API key on the critical path. The whole thing runs on one laptop with the wifi off, which is worth demonstrating.

Order of work

  • Simulator emitting clean multi-sensor streams — nothing else can be tested until this is honest
  • Dead-reckoning integrator and the L2 residual
  • Walk-off injection; confirm the residual actually separates
  • Console with the dual-track map — the moment the project becomes demonstrable
  • L1 and L4, then fusion and hysteresis
  • Fallback switching and the measurement card
  • L3, the report layer, and rehearsal

Where teams lose this

  • Perfecting the simulator's physics instead of building the detector. It only has to be plausible.
  • Tuning thresholds against the attack until it trips, then never measuring a clean run.
  • A beautiful console with nothing behind it, or a strong engine with no visible output.
  • Leaving the manoeuvre scenario until last and discovering the detector flaps.
  • No rehearsal. A live demo that has never been run end-to-end will not survive the room.
