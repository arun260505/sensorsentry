The demo cannot be hardcoded, and here is the reason

This is an architecture decision, not a talking point. Build it this way and the proof is structural — you point at the screen instead of arguing.

Keep that panel visible in a corner of the screen for the whole demo. When somebody asks whether it is hardcoded, you do not argue — you point at it and say: "That is everything the detector gets. Show me the field that tells it an attack is happening."

That usually ends the question in one sentence, because there is nothing to debate.

Setup

What is on the screen

One laptop, one layout, never changed during the demo. Plus a phone in a judge's hand.

THE MAP · biggest panel Two paths drawn live — the position GPS is claiming, and the position the vehicle's own sensors say. The gap between them is the whole story.
VERDICT PANEL Which sensor is guilty · attack, fault or interference · the recommended action in one sentence.
TRUST BARS One bar per sensor, sliding smoothly. Never jumping.
RAW FEED The incoming sensor message, scrolling. Your proof panel — always visible.
CONTROLS Scenario list · attack type · strength slider · direction · Start · Reset.

Hand the phone to a judge before you begin, showing the fleet app. Say nothing about it. When the attack fires and it buzzes in their hand, that lands harder than anything you could say about it.

Run of show

Six scenes, in this order

The order is deliberate. Credibility first, cleverness second, the finale last.

0:00 – 0:20

Open

Kill the hardcoding question before it is asked
Do

Point at the two windows, then at the raw feed panel.

Say

"Two separate programs. On the left, a simulated vehicle producing sensor readings. On the right, our detector."

"They talk over a network connection, and this panel here is everything the detector receives. There is no field in it that says an attack is happening, and it never sees the true position. So it cannot cheat — the information isn't there."

0:20 – 1:05

Scene 1 · Normal flight, no attack

Prove it does not cry wolf
Do

Run the clean scenario. Deliberately fly hard — sharp turns, a climb, a hard brake. Let it run a full 45 seconds with nothing happening.

Say

"Normal flight first. I'm turning hard, climbing, braking. Watch the trust bars — they dip and they recover. No alarm."

"Before I show you it catching anything, I want you to see it staying quiet. A detector that fires during ordinary manoeuvres gets switched off by its operator in a week, and then it protects nobody."

Do not rush this scene. Every judge is silently wondering whether you built an alarm that goes off constantly. Answering that before they ask puts everything after it on solid ground.

1:05 – 2:05

Scene 2 · Slow GPS spoofing

The core catch — and hand over control
Do

Turn the laptop toward a judge. Let them pick the direction and press Start.

Say

"You start it. Pick a direction — north, south, east, west. And you choose when."

Do

Say nothing for the first 8 seconds. Let them watch nothing happening.

Say

"Nothing looks wrong yet — and that's the point. This attack moves the position a couple of metres a second, so every individual reading looks completely reasonable."

Do

The paths separate on the map. The alarm fires. The phone buzzes in the judge's hand.

Say

"There. GPS named as the liar. Classified as an attack, not a fault. And look at the map — the vehicle is still on the real route. It stopped believing GPS and switched to its own motion sensors."

2:05 – 2:50

Scene 3 · A broken sensor

Your strongest moment — do not skip it
Do

Run the fault scenario. Same kind of symptom as scene 2. Point at the verdict panel as it changes.

Say

"Same kind of symptom — a sensor disagreeing with the others. But watch the verdict: sensor fault, not an attack. Complete the trip, service the vehicle tonight."

"That's a completely different instruction. If we'd called this an attack, you'd have grounded a healthy fleet for a failing compass. Every other system I know of stops at 'anomaly detected' and leaves that decision to a human."

2:50 – 3:20

Scene 4 · A magnet near the compass

Cover the third problem in the PS
Do

Run the interference scenario. Point at the compass trust bar falling while the others hold.

Say

"The compass swung — but the gyroscope felt no turn at all. So the vehicle did not rotate. The magnetic field around it changed. Somebody put a magnet next to the sensor."

"That's the third thing the problem statement asks for — manipulated environmental inputs. Same engine, no special case for it."

3:20 – 4:05

Scene 5 · A cargo truck

It is not a drone project
Do

Switch vehicle profile to truck. Run the theft scenario. The fake path carries on down NH-48 toward Bangalore; the real one turns off at the Sriperumbudur junction and stops in a yard off the Oragadam estate road.

Say

"Different vehicle. I changed nothing in the detector — I only told it this vehicle has wheels and a road map instead of a barometer."

"The control room screen shows a container making steady progress toward Bangalore. That lorry is parked in a yard off Oragadam Road being unloaded. Nothing in the tracking system is broken — the receiver is being lied to before the position is ever transmitted, and everything downstream is a very reliable pipe for the lie. This is how cargo actually gets stolen, and it is why the tracker is no help: it becomes the thief's best tool."

Expect

The truck alerts at about 47 seconds and says cannot isolate for the next twenty. It settles on gnss / attack around 65 s. That gap is not a glitch — say it before the judges see it, or it reads as one.

Say

"Watch what it does there. It has told you something is wrong the moment it knows something is wrong — and it is refusing to name a culprit until the evidence actually supports one. Twenty seconds later it commits: the GPS, and it is an attack. Most systems would have guessed at second one and had a 50/50 chance of blaming the wrong sensor. We would rather be slow and right, and we would rather say 'I don't know' than be confidently wrong."

4:05 – 5:05

Scene 7 · Four vehicles at once

The finale — nobody else will show this
Do

Run the fleet scenario. Four trucks in one area, hit together. Wait for the attack-zone circle to appear on the map.

Say

"Four trucks in the same area. Individually, any one of them could be a faulty sensor. All four at the same moment in the same place — that is an attack, and now we're certain."

"And here is the part a single-vehicle system cannot do at all. That circle is roughly where the transmitter is standing. Every other vehicle heading toward it has just been warned to reroute."

"One vehicle can be fooled. A fleet can't."

5:05 – 5:30

Close

Land on numbers, then on the business
Do

Bring up the results card.

Say

"Eight scenarios, one engine, nothing special-cased for any of them. Nine attacks out of nine caught, average sixteen seconds from the moment the attack starts, and zero false alarms across nine honest flights and drives — including hard manoeuvring."

"Everyone else solves this by fitting a special antenna to every vehicle, which is why almost nobody has it. Ours is software. It installs into the vehicles that are already on the road today."

If you only get 90 seconds: run the Open, Scene 1 (shortened to 20 s), Scene 2, then Scene 6. Skip everything else.
Proofs on demand

Six things you can do live if they still doubt it

Each takes under thirty seconds. Offer them before you are asked — volunteering a proof is far more convincing than defending one.

Proof 01

Run the same attack twice

The detection time comes out slightly different each run — 14 seconds, then 16 — because the sensor noise is randomly generated. A hardcoded demo gives the identical number every time. This is your cheapest and strongest proof.

Proof 02

Set the attack strength to zero

Press Start with the slider at zero. Nothing happens, no alarm, ever. If the alarm were on a timer it would fire anyway.

Proof 03

Let them choose everything

When it starts, which direction, how strong. Hand them the mouse. A script cannot respond to a moment somebody else picks.

Proof 04

Slide the strength up and down

Detection time changes smoothly with attack strength. Strong attacks are caught fast, gentle ones slowly. That continuous relationship is physics — scripts do not behave like that.

Proof 05

Show the code

Open the detection function for ten seconds and let them read it. It is short arithmetic. There is no if time == 90 anywhere in it.

Proof 06

Turn the wifi off

Do it at the start, in front of them. Nothing calls out to anything. The whole system is on that laptop.

The move that wins the argument

Show them where you fail

A sharp judge will still say: "but you wrote the simulator — you could have made the attack easy to catch." The answer is not a defence. It is an offer.

Turn the attack strength down step by step, live, and narrate what happens:

Attack strengthResultWhat it means
5 m/scaught ~15 s after onsetAggressive, and obvious to us
3 m/scaught ~16 s after onsetA thief in a hurry
2 m/scaught ~17 s after onsetOur limit on a drone
1 m/snot caught on a droneSlower than our own inertial drift
1 m/s, truckcaught, but it takes 159 sWheels and the road network see what a drone cannot
0.5 m/snot caughtBelow the floor on either vehicle
Say this out loud

"Below about 2 metres per second on a drone, the attack is slower than our own inertial drift and we genuinely cannot separate the two. That is our floor, and it is a property of the accelerometer rather than a bug we will fix later. On a truck we get down to 1, because wheels measure real distance and a lorry has to be on a road — neither of which a transmitter can reach."

"And the two floors fail differently, which is the more interesting half. Watch both for the same three and a half minutes: waiting longer catches a slower attack on a truck, and never does on a drone. A road stays where it is, so the attack eventually walks across a fixed line. A drone's only witness is inertial, and its uncertainty grows at the same time the attack does — so it never crosses. The drone floor is physics. The truck floor is only patience."

"It's also worth saying what that limit costs an attacker: at that speed, he needs about three hours to move the vehicle one kilometre."

Nobody fakes a demo that shows its own failure boundary. A team claiming they catch everything is obviously overselling, and experienced judges know it. A team that names the exact point where their system stops working is obviously real.

This is the single most credible thing you can do in that room. Volunteer it — do not wait to be pushed into it.

Questions you will get

Answers, close to word for word

Is this hardcoded? Did you just script the alarm?

"The detector is a separate program. It only receives sensor numbers — that panel there is the live feed. Nothing in it says an attack is happening, and it never sees the true position. You pick when the attack starts, how strong, and which direction. Run the same one twice and you'll get a different detection time, because the noise is random. Want to try it?"

But you wrote the simulator. You could have made this easy.

"Yes — which is why let me show you where we fail." Then run the strength ladder above. "Below about 2 metres per second on a drone we don't catch it, because that's slower than our own drift. On a truck we get to about 1, because the wheels and the road network see things the drone can't. Those numbers come out of a script, not a slide — I can run it now."

What if the vehicle just turns sharply? Won't that fool it?

"That was scene one — I flew it hard on purpose and nothing fired. Trust falls gradually and needs sustained disagreement before it acts, so a single manoeuvre can't trip it. Want me to run it again and turn harder?"

What if the attacker fakes all the sensors, not just GPS?

"He'd need physical access to the vehicle. A radio only reaches GPS — the motion sensor, the barometer and the wheels are inside the machine and don't listen to anything. And if he already has physical access to the vehicle, he doesn't need to spoof it, he can just take it."

Why not use AI or machine learning for this?

"Two reasons. It has to run on the vehicle at sensor rate, offline, and give the same answer every time it's replayed for an investigator — physics does that, a model doesn't. And training would need a large set of real spoofing attacks, which nobody has. An AI model does have one job here: writing the incident report afterwards, and there's a switch to turn that off."

Does it work on real data, or only your simulator?

"The detector takes an ordinary sensor stream — the same fields any flight log or telematics box produces. The simulator is our test tool, not our product. Point it at a real log and nothing about the detector changes."

What if GPS is fine and it's your motion sensor that's drifting?

"That's exactly why we don't compare GPS against one reference — we score every sensor pair and look for the one that disagrees with everyone. If the motion sensor were the bad one, it would be the one outvoted. And when two sensors are in conflict at once, the system says 'cannot isolate' rather than guessing. It's allowed to admit it doesn't know."

Preparation

Before you walk in, and if it breaks

Night before

  • Run all six scenarios end to end, five times, without stopping.
  • Confirm Reset returns to clean state in under two seconds.
  • Confirm the clean run still shows zero false alarms.
  • Charge the phone. Load the fleet app on it and leave it open.
  • Open the code file in a tab, ready to show.

Minutes before

  • Turn wifi off — in front of them if you can.
  • Close every notification, chat app and email client.
  • Increase font sizes; the back row must read the verdict panel.
  • Plug in power. Do not trust the battery.
  • Start both programs and leave them at the clean scenario.

If something breaks

  • Press Reset and keep talking. Do not apologise repeatedly.
  • Move to the next scene; come back if there is time.
  • If the whole thing dies, explain the architecture at the screen — the diagram carries the idea on its own.
  • Have a screen recording as backup, but never present a recording as if it were live. Say plainly that it is a recording.

Rehearse the whole thing five times minimum, out loud, including the words. A demo that has never been run start to finish will break in the room — that is not bad luck, it is the normal outcome. And knowing your lines is what lets you stay calm enough to hand a judge the mouse.
