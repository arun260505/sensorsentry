Reading the problem statement properly

PS 18 asks for three problems, not one

Most teams will read it as "detect GPS spoofing" and stop there. The sentence actually names three separate things — and covering all three is what makes our system different.

"GPS spoofing"

Someone fakes the signal

An attacker broadcasts counterfeit satellite signals. The vehicle receives a completely fake position and has no way to object.

"False readings"

A sensor is simply failing

No attacker at all. A sensor is old, damaged, or drifting. It reports wrong numbers with total confidence, exactly like an attack would.

"Manipulated environmental inputs"

Someone changes what the sensor feels

The attacker never touches the radio. He changes the physical world around the sensor — a magnet near the compass, heat near a thermal sensor, air pressure near the altitude sensor.

All three look identical at first — "something is wrong." Telling them apart is the hard part, and it is the reason this is a real product rather than a script. A failing sensor means service the vehicle. An attack means someone is nearby targeting your fleet. Opposite responses, so guessing is not good enough.

The problem in plain words

GPS is a whisper, and anyone can shout over it

A vehicle knows where it is by listening to satellites in space. Those satellites are extremely far away, so their signal arrives very weak — like someone whispering to you from across a field.

A person on the ground with a cheap radio box can simply shout louder than the whisper, pretending to be the satellite. The vehicle listens to the louder, clearer voice, and now believes a completely fake location.

Think of it this way

Someone swapped all the road signs. You follow every sign perfectly — and still end up in the wrong city.

You did not make a mistake. You trusted the sign, and the sign lied. That is exactly what happens to the vehicle. And the worst part: it has no idea. It is completely confident, and completely wrong.

What it costs when it happens

Two situations, the same attack

Case A · Medicine delivery drone

A hospital sends medicine to a village 12 km away

  1. An attacker parks near the route and switches on his box.
  2. The fake position starts only a few metres off. Nothing looks wrong.
  3. The drone thinks it has drifted, so it steers to correct — which actually pushes it further off the real route.
  4. Five minutes later it is 2 km from where it believes it is.
  5. It lands in an empty field. The attacker collects it.
  6. The flight log recorded the fake path, so the record shows a normal flight.

Drone gone. Medicine gone. No evidence for the company or the insurer.

Case B · Cargo truck

A container truck on a long-distance route

  1. A spoofer is placed in the cab, or beside the road.
  2. The control room's screen keeps showing the truck moving normally along its route.
  3. The real truck turns off and is unloaded somewhere else entirely.
  4. Nobody is alerted, because nothing on the screen looks wrong.
  5. Delivery records, toll records and driver-hours records are all now false.
  6. The insurance claim has no trustworthy location history behind it.

Cargo stolen, and the tracking system was the thief's best tool.

Our idea

The vehicle has other senses. Nobody is asking them.

GPS is not the only thing on board that knows something about where the vehicle is and what it is doing. Every one of these is already fitted — and none of them listens to a radio.

Sense 1

An inner ear

Feels speed, turning and tilting. Like walking across a dark room with your eyes closed — you still roughly know where you have reached.

Sense 2

A pressure sensor

Measures air pressure, which tells it how high it is. Nothing to do with satellites.

Sense 3

A compass

Knows which direction the vehicle is facing, from the Earth's magnetic field.

Sense 4 · road vehicles

Wheels and the map

The wheels count real distance travelled, and a truck must physically be on a road. Both are powerful checks a drone does not have.

The single question the software keeps asking

GPS says "you are here." Do the other senses agree?

Normally they all agree. But when someone fakes the GPS, the GPS starts describing movements the vehicle never actually made. GPS claims "you just moved 50 metres east" — the inner ear felt no eastward movement, and the wheels turned barely at all. That disagreement is the proof.

And this is why it works: an attacker can fake a radio signal arriving from far away. He cannot fake what the vehicle physically feels inside its own body. To beat us he would have to fool every sensor at once and keep them all perfectly consistent with each other — a far harder problem than shouting over a whisper.

The workflow

Ten stages, running continuously

Stages 5 and 6 are the ones nobody else builds. Everything before them exists to make those two possible.

1

Take in the readings

All sensor data arrives and is lined up in time, so every sensor's value for the same moment sits together.

From here on, the system does not care whether the data came from our simulator or from a real vehicle. Same input, same behaviour.

2

Check each sensor on its own

Before comparing anything, ask of each sensor alone: is it frozen on one value? Is it jumping around more than it should? Is it reading something physically impossible? Has it gone silent?

This step alone catches plainly broken sensors — the PS's "false readings" — before they can confuse anything downstream.

3

Build an independent witness

Using the inner ear, compass, pressure sensor and wheels — and deliberately not GPS — work out separately where the vehicle should be and which way it should be pointing.

This is the second opinion the attacker cannot reach, because it is built entirely from what the vehicle physically felt.

4

Cross-validate everything against everything

Do not just compare GPS against one reference. Compare every pair of sensors that can be checked against each other, and score how well each pair agrees. You end up with a complete picture of who agrees with whom.

"Cross-validates" is the word the problem statement uses. This stage is that word, built properly.

5

Find out which sensor is lying

Unique

If one sensor disagrees with every other sensor, while all the others agree with each other, that one sensor is the guilty one. The rest form a majority against it.

Most systems only say "something is wrong." Ours says "the compass is wrong, and here is the evidence." You cannot respond correctly until you know which sensor to stop trusting.

6

Decide: attack, or breakdown?

Unique

The same symptom has three possible causes, and they leave different fingerprints. A failing sensor behaves randomly and incoherently. An attack behaves smoothly and purposefully, pulling steadily in one direction, because the attacker wants the vehicle somewhere specific. Environmental manipulation shows up in one physical measurement while everything else stays honest.

This is the stage that covers all three problems the PS names, and it is the one that decides what happens next — service the vehicle, or raise the alarm on an attack in progress.

7

Score the trust, slowly

Each sensor gets a trust score that moves gradually — 100% to 70% to 20% — and the problem must persist before the system acts on it.

A single odd reading during a sharp turn is not an attack. A system that cries wolf gets switched off by its operator within a week, and then protects nobody.

8

Re-fuse, and keep going

The untrusted sensor is dropped from the calculation and the honest ones carry the navigation. The vehicle holds its true route and reports honestly how fast its accuracy is decaying, so the operator knows how long it can continue safely.

This is the "fusion" in the problem statement — a blend that changes according to trust, instead of a fixed recipe. Detecting an attack and then still following the fake position would be theatre.

9

Record the evidence

Store what happened: when it started, which sensor failed, which checks caught it, whether it was an attack or a fault, and the real path against the faked one.

This is what the company, the insurer and any investigation actually need — and in both stories above, it was the missing piece.

10

Show the operator

A live map with both paths, a trust score for every sensor, and a plain statement of what is happening and what the system already did about it.

The operator should not need to interpret numbers during an incident. The screen should say the answer.

Why so many pairs? Every pair is an independent witness. An attacker might defeat one check. Defeating all of them at the same time, while keeping them consistent with each other, is a completely different order of difficulty — and it is the reason this approach holds up.

Inside stage 6

Three causes, three fingerprints, three responses

CauseHow it behavesTelling signWhat we do
Sensor fault Random, jumpy, incoherent. No pattern and no purpose. The error goes in no particular direction, and often the sensor also fails its own health check. Drop the sensor, keep operating, flag the vehicle for service.
Spoofing attack Smooth, steady, purposeful. Pulls consistently in one direction. Too clean to be a fault. Real hardware fails messily; an attacker wants a specific destination. Alarm the operator, switch navigation, preserve evidence, check the rest of the fleet.
Environment manipulated One physical measurement shifts while everything else stays honest. The compass swings but the gyroscope felt no turn — so the vehicle did not rotate, the magnetic field around it changed. Ignore that sensor, warn that interference is present near the vehicle.
Novelty

Four things that make this ours

The vulnerability is well known and the physics are public. Novelty is not in noticing that GPS can be faked — it is in where the defence sits and what it does once it fires.

01 · No hardware

Software into vehicles already in service

Today the real protection is a special antenna fitted to each vehicle. It works, and almost nobody has it — because it means opening every unit and paying per unit. The hardware answer protects the fleet you buy next year. Ours installs into the fleet that is running today, using the sensors already on board.

02 · Names the sensor

A diagnosis, not an alarm

Not "something is wrong" — "the compass is lying, and the other four sensors agree that it is." That difference decides whether the operator can act. An alarm with no subject leaves a human guessing during the worst possible minute.

03 · Attack or fault

The question everyone else skips

Others stop at "anomaly detected." We answer why, because a broken sensor and a deliberate attack demand opposite responses. It is also the only way one system can cover all three problems PS 18 lists instead of just the first.

04 · The fleet checks itself

One vehicle can be fooled. A fleet cannot.

If one vehicle reports a GPS problem, it might be a fault. If five vehicles in the same area report it at the same moment, it is certainly an attack — and you now know roughly where the attacker is standing. Detection gets stronger as the fleet grows.

Why claim 04 is also the business

Every new customer makes the product better for every existing customer, because each vehicle added is another witness on the map. A competitor can copy our code in a month. They cannot copy the fleet.

What we build

Three pieces, all software, one laptop

No vehicle, no hardware, no internet needed. The problem statement says simulated sensor data — so the simulator is expected, not a shortcut.

Piece 1 · our test tool

The pretend vehicle

Runs an imaginary route and produces the sensor readings a real vehicle would produce. We also use it to launch attacks, faults and interference whenever we want.

Piece 2 · the product

The detector

Watches those readings and runs the ten stages — cross-checking, naming the guilty sensor, deciding attack or fault, and re-fusing what is left.

Piece 3 · the surface

The console

A live map with both paths — where GPS claims the vehicle is, and where it really is — with every sensor's trust score falling as the attack proceeds.

The sentence to say in the demo

The pretend vehicle is our testing tool, not our product. The detector accepts ordinary vehicle data, so pointing it at a real flight log or a real truck changes nothing about how it works.

Build one scenario with no attack in it at all. Fly a hard turn, or take a sharp corner, and show that no alarm fires. Judges will ask whether sudden manoeuvres fool the system, and proving it stays quiet when nothing is wrong is more convincing than catching one more attack.

Optional integration

Where an AI model is allowed to sit

Nothing in the detection path calls an AI model. This is physics and arithmetic, and an AI model is simply the wrong tool for it. Saying that plainly is stronger than apologising for a constraint.

It earns exactly one job: turning an incident into a report a human reads. The system already produces the facts — which sensor, which checks failed, by how much, when it started, attack or fault. Writing that up in clear sentences for the operator and the insurer is a genuine language task, and it happens after the event, not during it.

Put a visible switch on the console. Turn it off on stage and re-run the attack: detection is identical, only the written report disappears. That one gesture answers the question every judge is holding — did you just call somebody's API? — better than any slide can.
